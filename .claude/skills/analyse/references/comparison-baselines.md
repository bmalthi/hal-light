# Comparison baselines

A difference between two periods can reflect an unusual starting point, an unusual
ending point, or an ongoing movement. Check the context before interpreting the
size or direction of the difference.

## Check the endpoints

Place both periods in a longer series using the same metric and population
definition. Start with enough comparable history to see whether either endpoint is
unusual; choose the span and granularity for the business cadence and data available.
An existing trend pull may already answer this. A two-period comparison does not
automatically require a forecasting or seasonality model.

If an endpoint stands out, investigate what could change the interpretation:
incomplete data, a calendar difference, a temporary disruption, or a persistent
change. A low observation alone does not establish an outage. Use supporting
evidence to distinguish these explanations, and examine segments when an aggregate
hides materially different patterns.

## Preserve the requested comparison

Report the actual requested difference. If an unusual endpoint distorts its meaning,
add a relevant contextual comparison and explain why it changes the interpretation.
Choose that baseline for comparability, state its constituent periods, and avoid
selecting it because it supports the preferred story.

Do not remove inconvenient periods solely because they look unusual. When an
adjusted comparison is justified, retain the raw result and explain what was
excluded or adjusted. If the available history cannot establish a typical level,
limit the conclusion accordingly.

**Example:** Orders rise after a documented checkout outage. The adjacent-period
increase answers the requested question; comparison with comparable pre-outage
periods helps distinguish recovery from growth beyond the earlier level. Neither
the increase alone nor its timing establishes how much the outage caused.
