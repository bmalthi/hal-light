# Attribution

Reason from how the outcome is produced. Distinguish what moved, how a change could
propagate, and what initiated it, without extending the explanation beyond the
evidence.

## Understand the process behind the metric

Build the smallest account of the system needed to explain the outcome: the relevant
inputs and populations, decisions and constraints, and how their results are measured.
Derive that structure from the question and available evidence. Use it to identify
what could produce the observed pattern and which observation would distinguish the
competing explanations; a fixed list of business layers is unnecessary.

Ask what substantively changed for the outcome and population being discussed.
A changed label, version, assignment, or component does not by itself establish a
change in behavior or performance. Offsetting components or redistribution can leave
the total unchanged while affecting particular groups. Distinguish changes in the
underlying process from changes in its measurement, and state whose outcome the
explanation addresses.

## Separate the claims

- **Observed movement:** the measured change in the outcome or population.
- **Mechanism:** the process through which a proposed change affects that outcome.
- **Trigger:** the event or condition proposed to have initiated the change. A
  mechanism may itself be a response to an earlier change.

A population shift describes what reweighted; it does not establish why. A response
to an upstream change can still affect the outcome: distinguish its contribution
from the initiating event instead of treating them as independent causes. Trace
further upstream when doing so could change the explanation or decision, and stop
where the evidence stops.

## Test the consequential link

For the proposed explanation, identify the link whose failure would change the
conclusion and use the available evidence to test it. Depending on the question,
that might involve affected versus unaffected groups, timing, a controlled
comparison, or whether the proposed mechanism predicts the observed pattern.
Choose the design for the question; no particular scan or method is required here.

Coincident timing and matching direction support a hypothesis but do not establish
causation. Concurrent changes may interact or share an upstream cause, so avoid
counting the same effect twice or forcing contributions into independent causes.
State which links are supported, which remain plausible, and what is unidentified.
A supported descriptive finding remains useful when its initiating cause is unknown.
