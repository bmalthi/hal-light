# Dates and cohorts

Event dates select populations. Time since entry determines how much opportunity a
cohort has had to produce an outcome. Resolve both before interpreting comparisons
that depend on them.

## Match the clock to the question

Identify the event that places a unit in the reporting period and the event that
counts as its outcome. Distinguish activity occurring during a period from outcomes
of units that entered during that period.

Completions in a reporting period can come from several earlier entry cohorts;
outcomes of one entry cohort can span several reporting periods. These views answer
different questions and need not reconcile. Label the population and date basis of
each view used, and account for those definitions before interpreting a difference
between their totals. Keep the denominator aligned with the eligible population.

## Distinguish conversion maturity from changes in outcomes

For completion counts, distinguish changes in inflow from changes in how entrants
progress. For conversion rates, consider population composition, completion speed,
eventual completion probability, and incomplete follow-up. These are possibilities
to distinguish when relevant, not an exhaustive list or a required set of analyses.
Earlier entry cohorts may explain current completions even when current inflow is
unchanged.

Maturity is relative to the outcome being estimated. A cohort can have enough
follow-up for a short-horizon conversion rate while its eventual outcomes remain
unknown. Faster completion can be a real effect without higher eventual conversion;
slower completion need not mean permanent failure. Check calendar or segment-specific
timing when it could change that interpretation.

## Account for incomplete observation and selection

An outcome not observed before follow-up ends is not necessarily a non-event forever.
A common observation horizon, older complete cohorts, or a time-to-event method
that accounts for censoring may fit the question. Choose accordingly and state the
assumptions, including whether loss of follow-up could be related to the outcome.

A directly observed fixed-horizon rate needs a meaningful horizon observable for
the eligible cohorts, including their latest entrants. Do not shorten it merely to
include fresh data if that changes the question. Measuring conversion by that horizon
among all eligible entrants differs from selecting only those who converted quickly. Early
completers may differ systematically from later completers and non-completers;
equal follow-up alone does not make populations comparable.

## Treat timing models as assumptions to examine

If using a historical completion profile, establish that its estimation handles
incomplete follow-up and that its population and conditions fit the current question.
A blended profile can mislead when the mix of groups with different timing changes.
Assess the relevant difference without automatically fitting a model per segment.

The process under investigation may itself change completion speed. A gap from the
historical profile can therefore be a finding, as well as a sign of model error.
Explain any refitting that changes the baseline interpretation rather than silently
absorbing the change being investigated. Keep observed outcomes distinct from
projected outcomes. No particular hazard model or projection is required.
